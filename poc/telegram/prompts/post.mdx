Write a short social-feed introduction to someone's work, for a reader who has never heard of it.
The reader should understand what was made, why it is useful, and where to explore it in seconds.
This is not documentation, a tutorial, a repository summary, or a report of your research.

Writing contract:
- Title: the project name and, only if needed, a few plain words explaining its purpose.
  If unnamed, use a descriptive title. A missing name or public link does not block publication
  when ownership, purpose and what was accomplished are clear; never invent a name or URL.
- Summary: one short sentence, at most 25 words and 220 characters, stating the practical benefit.
- Detail: two or three short sentences, at most 70 words and 700 characters. Add useful context
  rather than repeating the summary: how it works in everyday terms, what the maker actually
  accomplished, and one material limitation or way to try it when supported.
- Explain specialist concepts through what they do. Prefer "compares runs with and without a
  skill" to "repeated, pinned A/B experiments"; do not assume readers know internal terminology.
- Simplify wording without inventing a guarantee or procedure. "Compares repeated runs" does
  not mean "repeats until an improvement appears". Preserve uncertainty and check relevant source
  caveats before describing a conclusion; an experiment can find no improvement or too little evidence.
- Omit commands, installation steps, file paths, configuration keys, internal document numbers,
  dependency versions, statistical procedures, API inventories, and benchmark minutiae. Readers
  can follow the source links for those. Retain a technical detail only when essential to
  understanding the benefit or a real access/compatibility limitation.
- Omit side conversations, insults, development gossip, design opinions, hype and inferred
  pronouns. Attribute unverified performance or cost claims to the maker; prefer leaving
  incidental numbers out. Say a project is experimental or unfinished when that matters.
  Preserve the scope of numbers: "a few hundred in one day" is not sustained daily throughput.
- Distinguish a design concept or illustrative results from a working system or measured results.
  Check the shared website itself before claiming what visitors can use or compare there.
- Describe instructions as guidance, not guarantees of agent behavior or proven improvements.
- Research verifies the introduction; it does not supply a checklist of facts to include.
  Before finishing, remove every sentence that doesn't help someone understand or try the work.
